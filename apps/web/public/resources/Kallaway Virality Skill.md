# Kallaway Virality Skill

A judgment framework for short-form video. It does not tell you which clips to cut — it tells you
whether the idea, hook, and structure are strong enough to be worth cutting, and what to fix when
they aren't.

## When to use it

- Choosing which story, take, or answer to build a short-form video around
- Writing or rewriting the first 3 seconds of any vertical video
- Deciding the order of beats in a cut, or fixing a cut that "goes flat in the middle"
- Reviewing a finished edit before export
- Reviewing a script, VO, or on-screen text the user wrote

Do not fire this for mechanical edits (trims, renames, colour, exports) or for long-form
where retention rules are different.

## The idea test

Every idea is **Topic + Seed + Substance**.

- **Topic** — the subject the video is about.
- **Seed** — the one-line premise or contrarian take that anchors the whole video.
- **Substance** — the facts, examples, or emotional beats that build around the seed.

Two questions decide whether it outperforms:

1. Is the topic relevant to the *ideal viewer*, not just to a large audience?
2. Are the seed and substance actually interesting or useful to that viewer?

Views from the wrong audience do not become leads or sales. When a user proposes a topic with
broad appeal but no fit to their buyer, say so before cutting.

## The hook is the whole video

Once someone scrolls past, nothing after that point matters. Short-form has no thumbnail, title,
or description — the hook is headline, intro, and pitch in one.

Budget **30-40% of total work on the hook.** Most people spend 5%.

### Three components, in order of importance

1. **Text hook** — what's written on screen. Processed first; eyes go to text. Matters most.
2. **Visual hook** — what's shown on screen. Stops the scroll or creates confusion.
3. **Spoken hook** — what's said. Least important: ~85% of short-form is watched without sound.

Most creators obsess over the spoken hook. Flip it.

**The first frame should be framed on whoever delivers the hook.** A wide group shot at frame 0
spends the visual hook on background and small faces. On a street-interview batch the client asked
for exactly this fix — "the first frame needs to zoom in" on the person saying the hook line — even
though every other shot in that series is a group-first two-shot. Punch in on the hook speaker for
the opening shot only; it also keeps the visual hook aligned with the spoken and text hooks.

### Max alignment

All three components must mean **the same thing**. A split-second of uncertainty between what is
said, shown, and written is enough to bounce a viewer — not because the content is bad, but
because they can't tell the time investment will be worth it. Misalignment is the difference
between 5K views and 500K on the same production quality.

### Every hook needs two things

- **On-target clarity** — within the first sentence the viewer knows what this is about and
  whether it's for them. Write at 5th-grade reading level, short direct sentences. If the first
  sentence doesn't tee up the subject, it isn't compressed enough.
- **A curiosity loop** — contrast between what the viewer currently believes and the new thing
  you're about to show. The bigger the gap between expectation and reality, the stronger the hook.

See [references/hooks.md](references/hooks.md) for worked good/bad alignment examples.

## The modern story arc

The school arc — conflict, rising action, climax, falling action, resolution — does not work on
social. Most people scroll away before the rising action gets good.

Use this instead:

| Window | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| 0-5s | Drop the viewer mid-story. Give ~70% context immediately. Never start at zero. |
| 5-15s | Spike to 90% intensity — the contrarian claim, the shocking stat, the turn. First local peak. |
| 15-45s | Release tension 10-15s, then another peak. Stack mini-peaks; rehook as often as possible. |

The climax is not in the middle. There is no single climax — there is a stack of peaks.

**Dropping in mid-story does not license dropping the context.** In a batch of sibling reels, each
reel still needs its own setup beat (who is asking, what is being shown, what the game is) right
after the hook — a viewer who meets reel 3 first has seen none of the others. One client's note:
"the intro, what product and every other context needs to be in there for all."

## Scriptwriting principles

Eight principles govern retention. Full detail in
[references/scriptwriting-principles.md](references/scriptwriting-principles.md).

- **Curiosity & contrast** — storytelling is popping a question in the viewer's head, answering
  it, and popping a new one, all the way down. All curiosity is rooted in contrast.
- **Speed to value** — attention is sand in an hourglass. Assume ~5 seconds to signal why to stay.
- **Value density** — raise the ratio of value to non-value. Cut every line the viewer doesn't
  need to hear.
- **Clarity & comprehension** — comprehension is the prerequisite to value. 6th-grade words,
  active voice, clean enunciation, say the best points twice (once precise, once stupid-simple).
- **Absorption rate** — maximise absorption, minimise implementation distance. Examples and
  frameworks that reframe a known idea absorb best.
- **Anticipation** — tease a condition so the viewer waits to see it pay off. Anticipation plus a
  non-obvious reveal is the strongest storytelling tactic available. In a reveal format (price
  guess, quiz) that means every guess plays before the answer and the answer lands once — a guess
  heard after the reveal kills the tension and reads as a mistake.
- **Emotional resonance** — pick the target emotion, then screen words, visuals, and audio for it.
  Fear and shock drive the most shares.
- **Rhythm, cadence & flow** — vary sentence length. Mostly staccato. Run-ons force the brain to
  delete older content to make room.
- **Tone & energy** — push energy above natural baseline; signal you want to be talking about this.

## The 11-point review

Run this against any cut before calling it done. Be honest — if you wouldn't watch it, they won't.

1. **Format** — is this the most compelling way to frame this information or story?
2. **Topic** — relevant to the ideal viewer, or only to us?
3. **Seed** — is the angle non-obvious? Does it challenge what they already believe?
4. **Substance** — is the takeaway tactically useful, or just entertainment?
5. **Visual hook** — contrast, movement, something unexpected? Is frame 0 framed on the hook
   speaker, or static and wide?
6. **Text hook** — does the on-screen text create curiosity and match the other two components?
7. **Spoken hook** — do the first 3 seconds give clarity *and* a curiosity loop?
8. **Story structure** — building anticipation, or listing facts?
9. **CTA** — is there a clear next action, or are we hoping they figure it out?
10. **Visual layout** — does the execution suit the presenter's actual strengths?
11. **Elements** — do captions, cuts, music, and pacing match the intended vibe? Fast content =
    fast cuts, animated captions, energetic audio. Slow content = longer holds, minimal captions,
    ambient sound. Energetic audio still sits under the speech — a bed the viewer notices over the
    words is a retention leak, not energy.

## Rules

- Fix the hook before touching anything else in a struggling cut. Reordering the body of a video
  whose first 3 seconds don't land is wasted work.
- Never let the three hook components drift apart to make one of them cleverer.
- When the user hands over a topic that's broad but off-target for their buyer, flag it before
  cutting rather than after.
- Report honestly which checklist points a cut fails. A recap that says everything passed is not
  a review.
- These are retention principles, not a house style. They do not override a brand's tone
  guardrails or a series' own format rules — where a client series says "no forced tears" or
  "don't mention the brand early", that wins.

# Hook Alignment — Worked Examples

## Good alignment (14M views) — Lifesize Floor Plans

- **Spoken:** "These are called Lifesize Floor Plans. You can literally walk through your home
  before you build it."
- **Visual:** People walking through full-scale floor plan models.
- **Text:** "Lifesize Floor Plans" / "Future of Home Design"

All three reinforce the same message. Impossible to misunderstand — the hook did its job.

## Bad alignment (40K views) — Space Cannons

- **Spoken:** "This company is sending shipping containers into space."
- **Visual:** Generic rockets in space, then a homemade PVC pipe.
- **Text:** "Space Cannons Are Awesome."

The spoken hook says one thing, the visual shows another, and the text adds nothing.

Same creator, same production quality, 350x difference in views. Alignment was the only variable.

## Building a curiosity loop

Curiosity = contrast. The formula:

1. Find a painpoint the viewer genuinely cares about.
2. Map their common belief about it.
3. State your contrarian reality — one you actually believe.
4. Frame why your reality beats their belief at solving that pain.
5. Give one fast hit of proof or a plain-language explanation.

Example: "If you want to save money on taxes, stop putting money in your 401K. Instead, open a
mega smart-yield harvest account." The 401K is the belief; the alternative is the contrarian
reality; the gap is the hook.

## How much leash the claim buys you

- The more outlandish the claim, the less time you have to justify it.
- The more confident the viewer is in their existing belief, the shorter the leash.
- The less they care about the topic, the shorter the leash.
- A viewer who cares deeply about the pain *and* isn't confident in their current answer gives you
  the longest leash of all.

## Emotional-story variant

For emotional and interview-led content, the same structure holds but the contrast is felt rather
than argued:

- **Belief:** the state the subject was in ("I cried for the first month").
- **Contrast:** the turn, told as one concrete scene, not a feeling.
- **Reveal:** what changed afterwards.

The hook should open on the strongest emotional line in the material — never on the interviewer,
never on setup.

## Game-format variant (price guess, quiz, blind test)

- **Text:** the most surprising guess as a big number on screen ("2,000/-").
- **Visual:** frame 0 punched in on the person making that guess, not the wide group.
- **Spoken:** the guess itself ("I guess 2000").

All three say the same thing — "someone just guessed this" — and the gap to the real price is the
curiosity loop the reveal pays off.

# The 8 Scriptwriting Principles

## 1. Curiosity & Contrast

The core retention driver. Storytelling is the art of popping a question in the viewer's head,
answering it, then popping a new one — all the way down.

Contrast is A vs B: expectations vs reality, what they thought vs what you think. All curiosity is
rooted in contrast. Aim for at least one major contrast moment per script; each additional
non-obvious claim you can position as true deepens the hook.

## 2. Speed to Value

Attention is sand in an hourglass — it starts running the second the video opens. Assume ~5 seconds
to signal a reason to stay. The more painful the problem you address, the more slack you get.

- **Entertainment / story:** value = something interesting worth hearing.
- **Education:** value = a non-obvious, tactically implementable solution.

You may not be able to *deliver* the value in 5 seconds. You must *signal* it.

## 3. Value Density

Raise the ratio of value to non-value across the whole runtime. If the viewer doesn't need to hear
it, cut it.

- Story content: how often something unexpected or interesting happens.
- Educational content: "walkaway value" — can they act on what you said?

## 4. Clarity & Comprehension

Comprehension is the prerequisite to value. Points that don't stick can't be acted on, so they
aren't valuable.

- **Word choice** — 6th grade or below. Terms are fine occasionally, defined in simple words.
- **Phrasing** — active voice ("the dog jumped"), not passive ("the jump was done by the dog").
- **Enunciation** — refilm stumbles and mumbles.
- **Repeaters** — say every valuable point twice: once precise, once stupid-simple with a metaphor.
- **Compression** — reduce each point to its atomic form. Every unnecessary word is one more thing
  the brain has to filter in real time.

Reframing test: can you make it two levels simpler and two levels more interesting?
("Claude Cowork is the future of productivity" -> "Claude Cowork lets your computer do work for you.")

## 5. Absorption Rate

Trust comes from the viewer successfully implementing your guidance. That depends on absorption
(did they understand it?) and implementation distance (how far to acting on it?). Maximise the
first, minimise the second. Examples and frameworks that make someone see a known principle in a
new way absorb best.

## 6. Anticipation

Reveal a detail or tease a condition that makes the viewer anticipate what's coming. The more
valuable the thing they're anticipating, the longer they hold. Anticipation paired with a
non-obvious reveal is the most powerful storytelling tactic available.

## 7. Emotional Resonance

Decide the emotion you're transferring, then screen every word, phrase, visual, and audio choice
against it. Common targets: joy, happiness, shock, fear, sadness, surprise, envy. Fear and shock
drive the most shares.

## 8. Rhythm, Cadence & Flow

Vary sentence length — a "trance rhythm" of short, medium, and long. Uniform sentence length reads
like a tumble dryer and puts people to sleep.

Online content works best mostly staccato. Short sentences let the brain hear, absorb, shift to
memory, and prepare for the next one. Run-ons force it to delete the oldest content to make room.

Never sacrifice clarity for rhythm.

## 9. Tone & Energy

Push energy above your natural baseline to signal you actually want to be talking about this. A
naturally punchy or deep voice needs less lift; a soft or monotone voice needs more.
